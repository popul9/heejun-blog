Went to the last .NET event of the year. If you want a meetup in Adelaide that goes really deep into tech, this one is in a league of its own.

The first time I went, I was a little overwhelmed by how technical it was, but even if you don't understand everything, after two or three visits similar themes come up, so you can follow along just fine :). Of course, these days it's mostly AI.

I try to approach problems with the user experience in mind, rather than starting with the technology (though I really do love that stuff). (And I won't deny having an engineer-centric mindset either.) But here, I can talk completely guilt-free about technology and hear firsthand from the people using it.

Which brings me to the best part of this meetup: actual engineers gather here. (There were three Microsoft MVPs here, which was WOW for me). On top of that, people leading those teams, the stakeholders around them, all come together. I had a chance to talk to people across quite a spectrum.

![Attendees listening to a talk at the Adelaide .NET meetup](./img_7611.avif)

To give a few examples,

- Adam Cogan, who leads SSW, an enterprise software development and consulting company.
- Freelancers
- Someone who left a stable, well-paid job to give a startup a go
- Developers working in defence
- Students & junior developers
- People training and developing AI models
- The Adelaide organisers who keep putting on local community events like .NET and DDD Adelaide, consistently, year after year.

So yes, I got to meet quite a variety of people.<br/>
Honestly, apart from money, is there anything more you could offer a developer than this? Ah of course, excluding free Domino's pizza.<br/>
I hadn't had dinner, so I really enjoyed the Domino's, but forgot to take a photo. Thanks to Simon, Encode Talent, and the sponsors for providing it 😋

What I paid particular attention to at this meetup was how developers' perceptions of AI are changing in software development right now, and the actual impact it's having.

It definitely feels like there are more projects, and less demand for people to work on them. And lately I've been interested in how we connect that stubbornness and pride we have in good design as developers/engineers with something clients can actually see the value in. So that's what I was listening for.
In other words, between Richard's [Worse is Better](https://dreamsongs.com/WorseIsBetter.html) and [Worse is Better is Worse](https://dreamsongs.com/Files/worse-is-worse.pdf), I'm happily going back and forth, just like Richard.. haha.

Oh and another software engineer was wondering whether AI would actually be useful enough to actively use at work, given the excessive overhead of code review. Watching him discuss this with the speaker, Adam Cogan, during the Q&A left an impression on me.<br/>
I went over to him after the talk and we had quite a long conversation. My question back was, whether it's a compiler, a runtime environment like the JVM or .NET runtime, or even how a CPU is implemented at the circuit level, we have to trust it and get on with development anyway. Fundamentally, being a software developer means having to trust some particular black box at some level, so isn't AI prompting fundamentally the same? So what I wanted to put forward was my view that a developer's preference for AI prompting might come down to comfort, whether the AI model and the intention behind the work are in sync, more than the AI's "intelligence" or "ability" to perform a task.

But then, it turned out he'd worked on a lot of defence projects, a completely different world from the one I work in. In environments and conditions where someone's life is on the line, with extreme security requirements, I could see how knowing the system inside out, even to a degree I'd normally consider excessive, would be a plus. After this conversation, I seriously thought about my own development environment and stack.

- I mostly do web development.
- The code I write mainly depends on browsers, or runtimes. (Java and C# are standard choices for web servers, after all. The magical JVM and .NET Runtime that compile code into native code and even optimise it for you as it runs.)
- Most of the development market is web, so the tooling is ridiculously helpful, from optimisation to catching mistakes at compile time and checking types. Even if a developer tries to break something, there are safety nets everywhere.
- In this situation, AI models have become specialised in these tasks. In Iron Man 1, he goes to Jarvis and says something like, work on the suit this way, and it comes out just right. In the world of web development, we've reached exactly that point, or passed it a long time ago.
- The complicated part of web development is ultimately the database. These days I also tend to give code a quick review and commit, but at least when there are changes involving entities, I review them two or three times. Again, you can fix code you've already deployed, but changing a data structure you've already established is really tricky. If I have a clear picture of the data relationships and structure in my head, however badly I mess up the code, as long as it compiles, I can fix it later (if I haven't tangled up the structure).

Anyway, we talked for quite a while, and towards the end, while talking about what we'd studied at university, we naturally got onto how long it had been since graduating. I knew he'd have more experience than me, but 20 years..! (I'm at two, anyway)<br/>
Hmm.. suddenly I felt so grateful that he'd listened carefully to the opinions of a second-year developer, excited to be somewhere he could talk all he wanted about IT and the developer experience.

- The developers for developers, who build the environment where my code can run around freely. More directly, all the MS MVP contributors who were there.
- The community meetup hosts who make a place for these gatherings and conversations.
- And the senior engineers who listen to my feelings and insights, and ask me about them, regardless of experience.
- Codex, which lets me run around wildly in the forest of development for USD 20 a month (I've become free to explore all sorts of questions, beyond just implementing code.)
- My workplace, where I get to build up so many insights while working.
- Finally, the pub that made all these interesting conversations possible, West Oak Hotel. (Sadly, I didn't take a photo.)

What else is ahead of me?<br/>
Of the people I met here, whose life looks closest to my future?<br/>
Far into the future, will I have the eyes of an engineer who can finally see the bigger picture?<br/>
Or will I still be happily lost in the undergrowth of development, busy solving the puzzles right in front of me?
